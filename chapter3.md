# Chapter 3: The Ancient Pact: Endosymbiosis and Eukaryotic Evolution

Why does every complex organism on Earth—every tree, mushroom, insect, and human—share the same double-membraned bioenergetic organelle, while not a single prokaryote has ever evolved multicelled morphological complexity on a macroscopic scale? To understand why mitochondria are built the way they are—why they possess cardiolipin-rich inner membranes, bacterial-style circular DNA, and an immune-triggering signature—we must examine the most consequential evolutionary event after the origin of life itself: the endosymbiotic merger that birthed the **Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor (LECA)** roughly 1.8 to 2.0 billion years ago.

## Two Domains Become One: Asgard Archaea and Alphaproteobacteria

For nearly two billion years after life emerged on Earth, the biosphere belonged exclusively to two prokaryotic domains: **Bacteria** and **Archaea**. Following the **Great Oxidation Event (GOE)** (~2.4 billion years ago), when oxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria began accumulating free $\text{O}_2$ in the oceans and atmosphere, metabolic partnerships between syntrophic microbes intensified.

Through phylogenomic analyses and deep-sea metagenomics, evolutionary biologists have reconstructed the two partners that fused to form the eukaryotic cell:

1. **The Archaeal Host (Asgard Archaea):** In 2015, Thijs Ettema and colleagues discovered **Lokiarchaeota** in marine sediments near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vent field in the Arctic Ocean, establishing the superphylum **Asgardarchaeota**. Subsequent cultivation of living Asgard archaea—beginning with *Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum* (Imachi et al., *Nature* 2020) and *Lokiarchaeum ossiferum* (Rodrigues-Oliveira et al., *Nature* 2023)—revealed small, slow-growing archaeal cells with long, branching membrane protrusions that live in intimate metabolic syntrophy with partner bacteria. Comparative genomics in 2023–2025 placed the origin of eukaryotes specifically within or sister to the **Hodarchaeales** lineage of Asgard archaea. Remarkably, Asgard genomes already encode hundreds of **Eukaryotic Signature Proteins (ESPs)**—including profilin-regulated actin cytoskeletons, ESCRT-III membrane-remodeling machinery, ubiquitin modifiers, and small GTPases—demonstrating that the archaeal host possessed nascent cytoskeletal complexity before or during its encounter with the mitochondrial ancestor.
2. **The Bacterial Endosymbiont (Alphaproteobacteria):** The proto-mitochondrion was a metabolically versatile relative of **Alphaproteobacteria** (closely allied with or basal to the Rickettsiales/Iodidimonadales lineages). Rather than being digested as prey, this bacterium established permanent residence inside the archaeal host.

## How the Merger Happened: Syntrophy and the Hydrogen Hypothesis

How did an archaeon come to internalize a bacterium? Two major classes of models explain the ecology of this merger:

* **The Hydrogen and Syntrophy Hypotheses (William Martin & Miklós Müller, 1998; Spang et al., 2019):** In anaerobic or microoxic environments, a facultatively anaerobic alphaproteobacterium fermented organic substrates, excreting molecular hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$), $\text{CO}_2$, and acetate as waste products. An $\text{H}_2$-dependent Asgard archaeon wrapped its long membrane extensions around the bacterium to capture this $\text{H}_2$ flux. Over evolutionary time, the host protrusions fused around the symbiont—matching the topology observed in cultured *Prometheoarchaeum*—generating a single cell with an internalized symbiont capable of both anaerobic syntrophy and aerobic respiration as environmental oxygen rose.
* **Aerobic Detoxification and Bioenergetic Coupling:** As oxygen levels climbed, the alphaproteobacterium's respiratory chain consumed toxic intracellular $\text{O}_2$ while generating a massive surplus of ATP. Once the endosymbiont evolved—or had co-opted—an **ATP/ADP translocase (ANT)** in its inner membrane to export ATP into the host cytosol in exchange for ADP, the symbiotic pact became irreversible.

## The Bioenergetic Dividend: Nick Lane's *Vital Question*

In his landmark bioenergetic analyses (*Nature* 2010; *The Vital Question*, 2015), UCL evolutionary biochemist **Nick Lane** and **William Martin** explained why bacteria and archaea remained microscopically simple for four billion years while eukaryotes exploded into macroscopic complexity.

The constraint is bioenergetic geometry. A bacterium respires across its outer plasma membrane. If a bacterium tries to expand its cell volume by a factor of 10,000 (the typical volume difference between a bacterium and a eukaryotic cell), its surface-area-to-volume ratio plummets. To produce enough ATP for a larger cell, the bacterium would have to pack its plasma membrane with respiratory complexes AND scale up its genome in thousands of polyploid copies right next to that membrane.

By internalizing bioenergetic membranes inside the cell in the form of thousands of folded mitochondrial cristae, eukaryotes broke the surface-area-to-volume barrier:
* **Internalized Power Scaling:** A eukaryotic cell can expand its bioenergetic membrane surface area exponentially simply by adding more mitochondria and folding their cristae, leaving its outer plasma membrane free for signaling, endocytosis, and cell-cell adhesion.
* **Energy Per Gene:** Freed from maintaining thousands of full bacterial chromosomes across a vast cell volume, the endosymbionts shed almost all their genes while the central nuclear genome expanded by orders of magnitude, funding luxury features such as spliceosomal introns, histone chromatin packaging, dynamic actin-tubulin cytoskeletons, and multicellular differentiation.

## Endosymbiotic Gene Transfer (EGT) and Why mtDNA Survived

A free-living alphaproteobacterium carries roughly 2,000 to 4,000 genes. Human mtDNA retains only **37 canonical genes** (including just 13 proteins). What happened to the other 99%?

Through **Endosymbiotic Gene Transfer (EGT)**, fragments of DNA released from lysed mitochondria were repeatedly integrated into the host's nuclear chromosomes. Many redundant bacterial genes were deleted outright, while hundreds acquired nuclear promoters and N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequences, allowing their protein products to be synthesized in the cytosol and imported back into the mitochondrion via the **TOM/TIM translocase complexes**.

Remarkably, this gene transfer is not merely ancient history. Using whole-genome sequencing of human tissues, **Patrick Chinnery** and **Martin Picard** demonstrated in *Nature* (2022) and *PLOS Biology* (2024) that **Nuclear Mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs)** continue to jump from mitochondria into the nuclear genome *de novo* both in the human germline (roughly once in every 4,000 births) and somatically across a person's lifespan—especially in the human prefrontal cortex, where higher rates of brain NUMT insertions correlate with earlier mortality.

Yet if the nucleus took over more than 1,100 mitochondrial proteins, **why did mitochondria retain a genome at all?** Why not move the final 13 protein-coding genes to the safe, well-repaired vault of the nucleus? Three complementary hypotheses—now strongly supported by biophysical evidence—explain why those 13 genes could never leave:

1. **The Co-location for Redox Regulation (CoRR) Hypothesis (John F. Allen, 1993; Nick Lane):** Because electron transport is a high-voltage process prone to generating lethal bursts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) if electron carriers become over-reduced, each individual mitochondrion—and even each local cristae neighborhood—must rapidly adjust the transcription and translation of core respiratory subunits in direct response to its own local membrane potential and redox state. If all respiratory genes resided in the nucleus, the cell could only broadcast a global, cell-wide response taking hours, rather than regulating individual organelles on the spot.
2. **The Extreme Hydrophobicity Hypothesis (Gunnar von Heijne; Iain Johnston & Ben Williams, *Cell Systems* 2016):** The 13 proteins retained in human mtDNA (such as ND1–ND6, CYTB, COX1–COX3, and ATP6/ATP8) are the most intensely hydrophobic, multi-pass transmembrane core subunits in the entire proteome. If synthesized in the aqueous cytosol, they fold prematurely or aggregate into toxic clumps before they can ever thread through the narrow TOM/TIM import pores. Instead, they must be synthesized on mitochondrial ribosomes docked directly on the inner membrane and inserted co-translationally via the **OXA1L insertase**.
3. **Genetic Code Divergence:** Because mammalian mitochondria reassigned the universal stop codon **UGA** to code for tryptophan (and **AUA** to methionine), a raw mtDNA gene transferred to the nucleus today would be prematurely truncated as nonsense by cytosolic ribosomes.

## Mitonuclear Co-Evolution and Speciation

Because all four proton-pumping complexes of oxidative phosphorylation (Complexes I, III, IV, and V) are **chimeras** assembled from both mtDNA-encoded core subunits and nuclear-encoded structural subunits (only Complex II is 100% nuclear-encoded), the two genomes are locked in a perpetual evolutionary dance known as **mitonuclear co-evolution**.

Animal mtDNA mutates roughly 10 to 20 times faster than nuclear DNA. To keep the respiratory chain functioning without electron leaks, nuclear genes encoding contacting OXPHOS subunits, mitoribosomal proteins, and mitochondrial aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases must continuously evolve compensatory amino acid substitutions that match the rapidly drifting mtDNA.

As evolutionary biologist **Geoffrey Hill** and geneticist **Douglas Wallace** have shown, when two populations of a species become geographically isolated, each population evolves its own private, coadapted pair of mitochondrial and nuclear genomes. If those two populations later hybridize, the offspring inherit nuclear chromosomes from both parents but mtDNA exclusively from the mother. The resulting **mitonuclear incompatibility**—mismatched respiratory subunits that fail to dock tightly—causes reduced ATP synthesis, elevated ROS leak, and hybrid breakdown or sterility (dramatically illustrated in marine copepods such as *Tigriopus californicus* and avian species boundaries). In this way, the two-billion-year-old pact between archaeon and bacterium continues to sculpt the origin of species today.
